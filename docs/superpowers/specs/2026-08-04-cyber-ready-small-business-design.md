## 1. Approved purpose, positioning and public copy

**Public brand:** Cyber Ready for Small Business.

**Audience:** Australian small and medium businesses across sectors, with a prominent specialist pathway for healthcare practices and providers.

**Service position:** practical, tailored cybersecurity training arranged after an initial conversation. The website must not state a fixed workshop duration, package, price, guarantee, accreditation, certification, response time, or outcome.

**Hero headline (exact):** “Practical cybersecurity training for Australian businesses.”

**Hero supporting message (exact):** “Help your team recognise threats, protect sensitive information and reduce the risk of compromise through practical, tailored training.”

**Primary call to action:** “Start a conversation” linking to `mailto:enzo491kw@gmail.com` with a prefilled subject such as `Cybersecurity training enquiry`.

**CTA supporting line:** “Tell us about your team, sector and the situations you want to prepare for. Training is scoped after an initial conversation.”

**Secondary CTA:** “Explore the Essential Eight” scrolling to the plain-English strategy cards.

The tone is calm, practical, capable and respectful of time-constrained Australian businesses. It explains risk without fear marketing and avoids implying that training alone makes an organisation secure.

## 2. Evidence-led opening and factual guardrails

### Required evidence block

Present the following as an attributed, compact opening panel labelled **“Why this matters in Australia”**:

- In FY2024–25, ASD’s ACSC received **over 84,700 cybercrime reports**, an average of **one report every six minutes**.[2]
- ASD’s ACSC responded to **over 1,200 cyber security incidents**, an 11% increase on the previous year.[2]
- The reported figures are not a national census: ASD states that the report uses two distinct datasets—ReportCyber cybercrime reports and cyber security incidents responded to by ASD’s ACSC—and that the data are point-in-time, self-reported indicators rather than all cyber threats in Australia.[1]
- The average **self-reported cost of cybercrime per business report** was **$80,850 overall**, **$56,600 for small business**, and **$97,200 for medium business**. These are reported-cost figures, not a promised or typical loss for every business.[2]
- ASD describes ransomware as the most disruptive cybercrime threat in FY2024–25 and says it can cripple an organisation’s ability to function.[1]

### Non-negotiable evidence rules

- Attribute cybercrime statistics and ransomware language to **ASD’s ACSC**, including the FY2024–25 qualifier.
- Preserve “over” for 84,700 reports and 1,200 incidents; preserve “average self-reported cost per report” for the cost figures.
- Do **not** claim a numerical average Australian SMB downtime, recovery time, or downtime cost. ASD does not publish a clean Australian SMB downtime/recovery metric in the approved evidence set.
- Do not turn national figures into a statement that every visitor is likely to be attacked, breached or extorted.
- Do not use “guaranteed”, “prevent”, “compliant”, “ASD-approved”, “government endorsed”, “best”, “leading”, or similar unsupported superlatives.

## 3. Information architecture and section-level requirements

1. **Header and hero** — brand wordmark, compact navigation, exact approved headline/supporting copy, primary and secondary CTA. Keep the enquiry action visible without a form.
2. **Evidence opening** — the required evidence block; include a source link adjacent to the statistics and a short “what these figures mean” explanation.
3. **Cybersecurity in Australia** — explain, in plain language, that businesses face scams, credential theft, business email compromise, ransomware and data risks; distinguish cybercrime reports from incidents ASD responded to.[1][4]
4. **Essential Eight, in practical terms** — eight equal strategy cards, then a note that the framework supports mitigation rather than a claim of complete security.[3]
5. **Healthcare, privacy and breach responsibilities** — a prominent specialist section with the distinctions in section 5 below.
6. **Tailored training** — a four-step service journey: initial conversation; relevant scenarios and roles; practical session design; next actions for the business. Do not assign durations, prices, bundled deliverables or an automatic audit.
7. **Incident-readiness basics** — a practical checklist and escalation prompts, not incident-response advice for a live breach. The ACSC small-business guide says training should be refreshed periodically and describes an emergency plan as a way to reduce impact and help staff act.[4]
8. **Source register, disclaimers and contact** — full mechanically generated source register, educational/legal disclaimer, ASD non-affiliation notice, enquiry CTA and contact email.

Suggested desktop navigation: `Why it matters` / `Essential Eight` / `Healthcare` / `How training works` / `Get in touch`.

## 4. Essential Eight strategy cards

Use all eight strategy names from ASD’s *Essential Eight Explained* (November 2023). They must appear once as the card headings, in this order, with a one-sentence plain-English explanation beneath each.[3]

1. **Patch applications** — Keep business applications updated so known weaknesses are less likely to be used.
2. **Patch operating systems** — Apply supported operating-system updates in a planned, reliable way.
3. **Multi-factor authentication** — Add a second check to important accounts, starting with email and administrator access.
4. **Restrict administrative privileges** — Give elevated access only where it is needed and review who has it.
5. **Application control** — Limit which applications can run so unapproved software is less likely to execute.
6. **Restrict Microsoft Office macros** — Control macros so unexpected documents are less able to run risky code.
7. **User application hardening** — Configure everyday software to reduce exposure to common attacks.
8. **Regular backups** — Maintain and test backups so important information can be recovered when needed.

Card content must remain educational. Do not publish maturity ratings, implementation targets, a self-certification mechanism, or a claim that a visitor meets the Essential Eight after reading the page.

## 5. Healthcare, privacy, NDB and My Health Record distinctions

### Healthcare specialist section

Open with the fact that health service providers routinely handle sensitive health information. The section is a specialist lens within the broader SMB service, not a statement that the service is limited to healthcare.[6]

Use a three-column distinction panel:

| Topic | Required treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Health privacy | Explain that the OAIC health-privacy guide is for health service providers’ obligations under the *Privacy Act 1988*. It must not state that every health practice has identical obligations or that training delivers legal compliance.[6] |
| NDB scheme | Explain that an eligible data breach has defined conditions: unauthorised access, disclosure or loss of held personal information; likely serious harm; and no remedial action that prevents that likely risk. Eligible breaches require notification to affected individuals and the OAIC. This is educational information, not legal advice.[7] |
| My Health Record | Keep My Health Record breach notification separate from the general NDB explanation. Reporting entities have notification requirements to the Australian Digital Health Agency for actual or potential My Health Record breaches; the Agency notes that non-My-Health-Record breaches may need to be handled under the Privacy Act NDB scheme, and that OAIC notification may also be required with exceptions.[8] |

Required healthcare data panel: **“OAIC data, not ASD data”**. State that the OAIC received 1,205 data breach notifications in calendar 2025; 716 involved malicious or criminal activity; health service providers accounted for 225 notifications (19%). Attribute every figure to the OAIC and do not re-label these notifications as ASD incidents, cybercrime reports, or a healthcare breach rate.[5]

Do not give legal conclusions, decide whether an incident is notifiable, set a reporting deadline beyond the source language, or claim that all healthcare providers are covered by the same privacy regime. Direct visitors to the OAIC and Australian Digital Health Agency source links for current obligations.[6][7][8]

## 6. Tailored-training and incident-readiness content rules

### Tailored training

Describe topics as examples to discuss after the initial conversation: suspicious emails and invoices, passphrases and MFA, handling sensitive information, device and software updates, reporting pathways, backups, and practising what to do in an emergency. The ACSC guide identifies training, common threats, protective measures and reporting processes as relevant staff-awareness topics.[4]

The design must make clear that final scope follows the conversation and can vary by business role, systems, data handled and sector. Do not imply an assessment, remediation service, penetration test, managed security service, legal advice, incident response retainer, or certification.

### Incident-readiness basics

Use a short checklist: identify who staff tell; retain key contacts; agree how internal communications work; identify critical systems and a business-as-usual fallback; keep an accessible copy of the plan; practise and refresh it. This adapts the ACSC small-business emergency-plan prompts into training content rather than presenting it as a complete incident response plan.[4]

For an actual or suspected incident, the page may direct visitors to current official reporting and recovery channels. It must not instruct visitors to delay emergency, legal, privacy, insurer or specialist incident-response advice.

**Required disclaimer copy:** “Cyber Ready for Small Business provides educational cybersecurity training. It is not legal advice, privacy advice, incident-response advice or a certification service. Cyber Ready for Small Business is not affiliated with, endorsed by or acting on behalf of the Australian Signals Directorate (ASD), the Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC), the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC), or the Australian Digital Health Agency.”

## 7. Visual design system

### Direction

Create a light editorial Australian-business aesthetic: considered, legible and human. Use grounded workplace cues—documents, calm abstract data patterns, daylight office details, or simple line illustrations—not hackers, code rain, hooded figures, locks floating in space, surveillance imagery or catastrophic breach imagery.

### Tokens

| Token | Specification |
| --- | --- |
| Background | Warm cream/off-white, approximately `#F6F1E8` |
| Primary text | Near-charcoal, approximately `#242522` |
| Accent | Restrained rust/copper, approximately `#A65A3A` |
| Supporting accent | Muted eucalyptus, approximately `#6F8879` |
| Surface | Light cream card, approximately `#FFFDF8` with a fine warm-grey border |
| Type | Editorial serif for display headings paired with a highly legible sans-serif for body and interface text |
| Layout | Generous whitespace, clear reading measure, 12-column desktop grid, single-column mobile flow |
| Imagery | Realistic or abstract, inclusive Australian small-business context; image alt text must communicate purpose, not decoration |

Use copper sparingly for emphasis and interaction states, not as large blocks of colour. Never rely on colour alone for meaning. Maintain WCAG 2.2 AA text contrast and visible keyboard focus.

## 8. Responsive and accessibility behaviour

- Mobile first: a single-column reading order; navigation collapses to an accessible menu; CTA remains easily reachable; cards stack without horizontal scrolling.
- Tablet: retain comfortable measure and two-column card groupings where space permits.
- Desktop: keep main content within a readable maximum width; allow the evidence panel and CTA module to create editorial rhythm without sacrificing scanability.
- Use semantic landmarks, one logical H1, ordered heading levels, descriptive link text, native buttons/links, skip link, visible focus, and `prefers-reduced-motion` support.
- Do not autoplay media, use timed carousels, conceal key text in images, or require hover to reveal content.
- Tables and comparison content must reflow or use an accessible stacked representation on narrow screens.
